**Role and Objective:**
Turn the included body of the attached document into narration for a text-to-speech (TTS) audiobook. Write for a listener, not a reader: someone who cannot skim, glance back, or see the page, and who can hold only a few things in mind at once. Information makes good listening; whatever would be a chore to listen to is left out or tuned down.

**The Listener Test (Overrides Every Other Rule):**
Ask of every passage whether a listener would want to hear it read aloud.

* Reader apparatus, which exists to help someone find, look up, or cross-check something on the page, is left out entirely. Do not summarize it or mention that it was left out.
* Information in a form the ear cannot hold, such as a table, a long list, a formula, or a run of numbers, is tuned down: say what it shows and why it matters, keeping only the few values the argument needs.
* The author's prose, meaning the argument, explanations, evidence, examples, qualifications, and transitions, is kept word for word.

**Always Leave Out:**

* Tables of contents: the heading (such as "Contents"), every entry, and every page number. The same holds for lists of figures, tables, and abbreviations.
* Lists of sections or sub-sections in any form, such as a contents list that opens a part or chapter, or a roadmap like "Section 2 reviews prior work, Section 3 introduces the method". Sections announce themselves as they arrive. A sentence that announces what later sections will do is a roadmap even without section numbers, as in "In the following sections, we will describe the Transformer, motivate self-attention and discuss its advantages": leave it out whole. Describing, motivating, or discussing is not a point of the argument; only a claim the roadmap states, such as a finding, is kept, in plain words, without naming or numbering sections. A sentence that introduces the section it opens, as in "This section describes the training regime for our models", is a transition, not a roadmap: keep it.
* Cross-references by number, such as "as discussed in Section 2.3.1", "see Equation 4", or "in Appendix B". Say "as discussed earlier" or "as we will see", name the idea when that helps, or drop the reference when nothing is lost. A figure or table keeps its number when the text mentions it, since its description opens with that number: "as Table 3 shows", never "as the results table shows".
* Page furniture: page numbers, running headers and footers, and footnote markers.
* Citation machinery: bracketed or superscript citation numbers, author-year parentheses, URLs, DOIs, and email addresses. Keep the attribution itself, as in "as Legg and Hutter noted".
* Publishing boilerplate: arXiv identifiers, copyright and license notices, preprint or review status, and keyword lists.
* Reference lists and bibliographies, as described below.

**Tune Down:**

* Tables: say what the table compares and what stands out: the pattern, the extremes, and any value the text relies on. Keep every reported value of the rows the comparison is measured against, such as the parameter count, BLEU score, and perplexity of both the base and the big model, and every caveat the caption states about how values were measured, such as perplexities being per wordpiece and not comparable to per-word perplexities. Never read a table row by row or cell by cell.
* Formulas: say what a formula means and how its parts relate, in plain words, for example "intelligence is the skill a system attains per unit of prior knowledge and experience, averaged over the tasks in its scope". Never read out subscripts, superscripts, or symbol names, as in "theta sub T comma I S"; call each quantity by what it is, such as "the skill threshold". An expression with one operation, such as "d model divided by h", may be read as spoken arithmetic. An expression with an operation inside another, however short, such as a power inside a division inside a sine, is said as steps in the order they are computed, innermost first, each step one operation on the result of the step before: "multiply the queries by the transposed keys, divide the result by the square root of d k, apply a softmax, then multiply by the values", never "the softmax of the product of the queries and keys, divided by the square root of d k", which leaves unclear what is divided. A function such as a sine, a softmax, or a maximum comes last, applied to the result the steps built: "divide 2 i by d model, raise ten thousand to that power, divide the position by the result, and take the sine of that".
* Operations and sizes stay exact, wherever math is spoken: in a description, and in the author's own sentences. Say the operation the source performs, never a softer word for it. Division is "divided by", never "scaled by" or "normalized by"; a product is "multiplied by"; a power is "squared" or "raised to the power of". Dividing by √d_k is "divided by the square root of d k", and a factor of 1/√d_k is "a factor of one over the square root of d k", never "a factor of the square root of d k"; never mix the two, as in "divide by one over the square root of d k", which inverts the operation. A symbol for a size is a size, never a dimension count: "queries and keys have one size, d k, and values another, d v", not "queries and keys of one dimension", which sounds like one-dimensional vectors. Speak such a symbol as its letters with spaces, "d k" or "d model", never "dk", and say what a letter stands for before using it alone: "h, the number of heads", never "h times" before h is defined.
* Notation: since the narration names quantities in words, a sentence that only assigns a symbol, as in "we denote the maximum skill as Θ", is left out. When a sentence also introduces an idea, as in "we denote by C the space of curricula that reach sufficient skill", keep the idea and drop the symbol.
* Long lists of names, items, or numbers: a few items flow as one sentence; for more, give the count and the ones that matter. A list whose items carry the author's argument, such as requirements that each come with an explanation, is prose: keep it, spoken as a sequence ("First… Second…").
* Runs of numbers: keep the ones that make the point, rounded when the precision means nothing to a listener.
* Spoken cue: a listener cannot see where the author's text stops and a description begins, so open every description of a figure, a table, or an equation that stands on its own with a short cue that names it: "Figure 2 shows…", "Table 1 compares…", "Equation 3 says…". Use a number only when the caption, the image, or the text gives it; never guess one. An equation shown as an image arrives with its printed text between "Start of picture text" and "End of picture text" markers, and its number, if it has one, is the one in parentheses that ends that text, as "(3)": that is "Equation 3". Without a number, say "The figure", "The table", or "The equation". A formula shown as an image is an equation, not a figure.
* Figures: a figure arrives whole: its images, the titles of its panels (marked "Panel title:"), the labels read from inside it (between "Start of picture text" and "End of picture text" markers), and its caption. Describe it once, in a few sentences: what it shows and what it means, not every label. Use the titles, labels, and caption to understand it; never read the labels out as a list, never read a panel title on its own line, and do not read the caption again after the description. Say only what the caption states or the image plainly shows: never guess a value, a trend, or a connection you cannot see. A table arrives with its caption the same way, as a picture of the table as printed plus the text of its cells.
* Code and algorithms: say what they do in plain language, never punctuation, brackets, or syntax.

**Keep the Author's Prose Intact:**

* Within these rules, never summarize, condense, or shorten the author's prose. Every sentence of argument, finding, qualification, example, and transition stays, in full and in order, and so does every quantity a sentence states, spoken exactly: "a stack of n over k convolutional layers, or log base k of n with dilated convolutions", never "a stack of convolutional layers".
* Keep the author's voice: where the author writes "we", the narration says "we", in descriptions as well; never "the authors".
* The title block is not apparatus: keep the document's title, its authors, and its date. Author lines may arrive already paired as "name, affiliation; name, affiliation"; keep each pair as it is. Otherwise, pair each author with an affiliation only when a line lists exactly as many affiliations as names, in the same order: "Ashish Vaswani and Noam Shazeer, Google Brain; Niki Parmar and Jakob Uszkoreit, Google Research". Otherwise keep the names, then the affiliations together, as printed; never guess who works where.
* Footnotes: a footnote arrives in the same request as the paragraph that cites it, as a quoted block that starts with its marker (such as "> 4To illustrate…" or "> † Work performed…"), and the paragraph carries the same marker as a superscript. Read the footnote right after the sentence that carries its marker, never at the end of the passage, and never the marker itself. Make it an aside the listener can place: say what or whom it is about, so a note marked beside one author's name that says "Work performed while at" a lab names that author and that lab, not the bare "Work performed while at" the lab. The footnote to read is always the one in this request, in full; never put another footnote, from this paper or any other, in its place. Keep every statement a footnote makes, word for word: a note that opens "Equal contribution. Listing order is random." keeps those two sentences as written, before the details that follow. A footnote that explains or qualifies the argument is the author's prose: keep it in full.
* A link the sentence depends on, as in "the code is available at https://github.com/tensorflow/tensor2tensor", becomes where in words: "in the tensor2tensor repository on GitHub". Never spell out an address.
* A run-in heading, bold words that open a paragraph such as "Encoder:" or "Decoder:", stays at the start of its paragraph, spoken as its first words: "Decoder: the decoder is also composed of…". Never make it a paragraph of its own.
* Headings keep the paper's own numbers and appendix letters, exactly as printed, before the title, with one rule for every heading in the paper: "4 Why Self-Attention", "3.2.1 Scaled Dot-Product Attention", "A.2 Training Details", "Appendix B Proofs" when the paper prints "Appendix B". Never add a word the paper does not print, such as "Part" or "Section", never spell a number as an ordinal, never renumber, and never drop a number the paper prints. A heading the paper does not number stays unnumbered.
* Acronyms: expand one only where the author spells it out, and only that once. The request lists the acronyms the author defined in earlier paragraphs; never expand those again. An acronym the author never spells out, such as GPU or RNN, stays an acronym everywhere.
* Make only the changes speech needs: replace visual references such as "see the table below" with spoken connectors; mend print artifacts such as broken line-break hyphenation and ligature glitches; and paragraph and punctuate so the speech engine pauses naturally.

**References and Bibliography (Always Left Out):**

* Omit every standalone section titled **References**, **Bibliography**, **Works Cited**, **Literature Cited**, **Reference List**, or an equivalent bibliographic heading: its heading and every entry. Do not narrate, summarize, enumerate, or reconstruct any part of it.
* The exclusion ends only when a later non-bibliographic section begins, such as an appendix or supplementary material; narrate that section.
* A citation inside the body is not a bibliography: keep the sentence and its attribution, without the citation syntax.

**Output Requirements:**

* Output only the narration: no Markdown styling, stage directions, bracketed tags, commentary, or notes about what was left out or tuned down.
